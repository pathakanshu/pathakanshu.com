Too stressed to start? How stress relates to procrastination

You've got an assignment due in a few days. There's absolutely no rush at all, plenty of time to get on with it. Instead you end up doing something else (watching something, scrolling on your phone etc). As the deadline approaches you find yourself having done absolutely nothing at all.

Most people would probably label this as poor time management, and on some occasions it absolutely is. What I'd like to consider though is another possible explanation for why we procrastinate. Could stress be the culprit?

What the study looked at

In 2023, researchers from a Chinese university investigated the link between stressful life events and procrastination. They surveyed 794 Chinese university students, asking them about their stressful experiences, their attitude to stress, and how they generally saw themselves.

One thing to note is that they weren't just looking at stress coming from schoolwork. Stressful life events could also include things outside of college itself (family problems, relationship problems, health issues, etc.) and would all be things that a student might be going through even if they were focusing on something completely different.

The researchers found that students who reported having experienced more stressful life events also tended to procrastinate more.

That in itself isn't all that surprising. What was more interesting, though, was that they also looked at how people think about stress and themselves rather than simply how much stress they'd experienced.

Some findings

The researchers looked at two factors which might help explain the relationship.

The first was stress beliefs. That's essentially what someone believes stress does to them. Some people view stress as completely bad, others see it as occasionally helpful. One person might see the pressure of an approaching deadline as a reason to get on with it, another might find it overwhelming.

The second was core self-evaluations (a rather more difficult concept to pin down). This is about how people generally see themselves, their self-esteem, their confidence and how much control they feel they have over their lives.

Both factors were found to be involved in the link between stressful life events and procrastination.

The researchers used mediation analysis to look at this. Rather than simply looking at whether stress and procrastination were related, they wanted to see if people's beliefs about stress and themselves might help explain that relationship.

Their analysis suggested that stress beliefs and core self-evaluations could each explain part of the link. They also found a possible link involving both of these factors together.

Stress may not be the whole story. How people understand stress and how capable they feel of dealing with things may also be important.

How does this relate to everyday life?

Think about a time when you've had a lot going on outside of college. Maybe you've been having problems at home or dealing with something that's been weighing on your mind. You've got an assignment due next week which you'd normally have no difficulty at all with.

You've been under a bit of pressure recently and may be finding it difficult to concentrate. There's a fear that you won't be able to do the work properly, so you just keep putting it off, even though you know that's going to make things worse.

Now think about how someone else in the same position might react. They might be more confident in their ability to handle the task, or they may not see stress as something that necessarily stops them from getting things done.

Neither person is necessarily experiencing less stress than the other. The difference might be in how they understand that stress and how capable they feel of dealing with it.

I think it's worth thinking about because procrastination is so often viewed as a problem with the individual. We tend to assume that someone who is procrastinating just doesn't care at all about their work, without really knowing what's going on in their life outside of that work.

But if two people experiencing similar pressures can respond to them differently, perhaps there's more to procrastination than simply how motivated or disciplined someone is. It might also be worth considering what they're dealing with and how they feel about their ability to handle it.

Conclusion and limitations

There are a few things to keep in mind when reading the study.

For one thing, the researchers gathered their responses at a single point in time. They found a link between stress and procrastination, but that doesn't prove that stress causes people to procrastinate.

The study also depended on the students reporting their own experiences and their answers may not have been completely accurate. The participants were all college students in China, so we can't be sure that people across the world would have responded in the same way.

The findings are still interesting in that they suggest procrastination is linked not just to stressful experiences themselves, but also to how people view those experiences and their own ability to deal with them.

I don't think that means time management and discipline don't matter. They obviously have a part to play. It's quite possible that someone's situation is a big part of why they procrastinate rather than them simply being lazy. That's something we should probably bear in mind before we jump to that conclusion.
